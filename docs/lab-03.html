Background

In this lab we will explore the impacts of tessellated surfaces and the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) using the National Dam Inventory maintained by the United States Army Corps of Engineers. The work requires writing reusable functions and careful consideration of feature aggregation/simplification, spatial joins, and data visualization. The end goal is to visualize the distribution of dams and their purposes across the country.

Step 1.3

Tessellations/Coverage’s describe the extent of a region with geometric shapes, called tiles, with no overlaps or gaps.

Tiles can range in size, shape, area and have different methods for being created.

Some methods generate triangular tiles across a set of defined points (e.g. Voronoi and Delaunay triangulation)

Others generate equal area tiles over a known extent (st_make_grid)

For this lab, we will create surfaces of CONUS using using 4 methods, 2 based on an extent and 2 based on point anchors:

Tessellations :

  • st_voronoi: creates a Voronoi tessellation

  • st_triangulate: triangulates a set of points (not constrained)

Coverages:

  • st_make_grid: creates a square grid covering the geometry of an sf or sfc object

  • st_make_grid(square = FALSE): creates a hexagonal grid covering the geometry of an sf or sfc object

  • The side of coverage tiles can be defined by a cell resolution or a specified number of cell in the X and Y direction

Why Different Cropping Methods?

The distinction matters for your spatial analysis:

  • st_intersection() on Voronoi/Delaunay: Produces edges/boundaries that align precisely with the CONUS border. Tiles at the edge are cut, not discarded. This is geometrically accurate but creates irregular edge shapes.

  • st_filter() on hexagon/square grids: Keeps only complete regular tiles that touch CONUS, discarding edge tiles. The boundary is a stair-step pattern following the regular grid.

In Q3, when you compare these tessellations, you’ll notice that Voronoi and Delaunay have denser tile coverage (e.g., ~2800–6200 tiles) because they extend to the exact boundary. Hexagons and squares have fewer tiles (e.g., ~1900–2000) because they’re bound to a regular grid. This difference affects spatial statistics: denser tessellations reveal finer-grained patterns; coarser tessellations show broader regional structure. This is part of the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP).

pinp <- function(pts, polys, id_col, colname = 'point_count') {
  
  if (st_crs(polys) != st_crs(pts)) {
    message("Transforming crs's to match")
    pts <- st_transform(pts, st_crs(polys))
  }

  joined <- st_join(pts, polys, join = st_intersects)

  counts <- joined |>
    st_drop_geometry() |>
    count(.data[[id_col]], name = colname)

  result <- polys |>
    left_join(counts, by = id_col) |>
    mutate(point_count = coalesce(point_count, 0)) # Replace NAs with 0
  return(result)
}

b. Identify 2–3 geographic regions where tessellation choice produces substantially different patterns (e.g., one method shows a clear hotspot while another shows diffuse distribution).

The voronoi polygons, triangles, and counties create a hotspot in Northwestern Montana that is not present in the grid tesselations. This is because the counties in Montana are large, so many dams are contained in one or two polygons. This creates a hotspot because the density of dams per polygon is high relative to the rest of the polygons.

In contrast, Appalachia is a hotspot in the gridded tessellations, but not in the irregular tessellations. This is because counties are very dense and small in this part of the country, and so most polygons in the irregular tessellations have very few dams. In the uniform tessellations, this area of the country has the most dams per area, and so a hotspot is visible.

c. Which single tessellation would you recommend to a dam operations manager seeking to understand geographic clustering?

The square gridded tessellation provides the best, most interpretable visualization for geographic clustering. Unlike Voronoi, triangles, or raw county boundaries, this tessellation is unbiased by the history of county boundary creations, conflicts, politics, population density, and natural features such as rivers and mountain ranges. It gives us a clear view of the country, allowing us to answer “where are dams most clustered?” without needing to consider how the county boundaries are affecting the visible patterns.

The irregular tessellations are not valid to understand geographic clustering. I cannot think of a use case for the Voronoi or triangle tessellations in the dam density context. The county polygon tessellation may be useful if you are specifically interested in which counties have many dams and if counties had jurisdiction over dams, but they typically don’t.

The hexagonal tessellation provides no clear advantage over the square and has the disadvantages of being unable to be converted into a raster format and being less common. The square tessellation best supports management decisions across many jurisdictional boundaries. However, we could adjust the size of our square grid based on the specific use case, as this may impact where clusters are visible.

Step 5.4

a. Spatial Clustering Patterns (Quantitative + Geographic)

Number of dams is strongly spatially correlated. This is because dams are concentrated in areas with wet climates, arable land, and people who need water. Deserts and areas with low population typically do not have large numbers of dams. Dam age is also correlated, although not as strongly as number of dams. As the population of the US moved West, they built dams when water resources became stretched thin between the people living there. Most dams were also built during periods when funding was large such as the 1930s Tennessee Valley Authority era. Dam size is not clustered. It’s unlikely a small area (such as one tile in our tessellation) can support many large dams. Also, our metric for investigating dam size is the mean surface area of the dams within each grid cell. So some spatial autocorrelation is likely lost in this averaging.

b. Moran Scatterplot Interpretation (Visualization)

Points generally cluster near the diagonal, suggesting positive spatial autocorrelation. There are a few notable outliers. The number of dams is a much stronger correlation than the age of dams. The surface area of dams is not spatially autocorrelated.

c. Spatial Structure Implications

The clustering is weak for dam surface area, moderate for dam age, and strong for number of dams. Management factors, the population expansion of the US, and the history of dam building drives the correlation in age and number of dams. Hydrologic factors are also visible as there are no dams in the Southwestern deserts and there are many dams in the East.
